A profitable business can run out of cash. A loss-making business can accumulate it. The cash flow report in aiGust makes this apparent by showing exactly where money moved in and out of the business during the period — separated into three activity types so you can identify whether cash is being generated by trading, consumed by investment, or supplemented by external financing. This is often the most revealing report you can show a client who thinks their P&L is the whole story.

## What the Cash Flow Report Shows

The cash flow report covers the same period as the P&L but measures actual cash movements rather than accrual-based income and expense. It is divided into three sections:

<CardGroup cols={3}>
  <Card title="Operating Activities" icon="gears">
    Cash generated by the core trading activities of the business. Starts with net profit and adjusts for non-cash items and working capital changes. A healthy business consistently generates positive operating cash flow.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Investing Activities" icon="chart-line">
    Cash spent on or received from long-term assets. Purchases of equipment, property, or investments appear as outflows. Proceeds from asset disposals appear as inflows. Sustained heavy investing cash outflows often signal a growth phase.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Financing Activities" icon="building-columns">
    Cash movements related to how the business is funded. Loan drawdowns and equity injections are inflows; loan repayments, dividend payments, and owner drawings are outflows.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

The three sections sum to the **net movement in cash** for the period. Adding this to the opening cash balance reconciles to the closing cash balance shown on the balance sheet.

## Direct vs Indirect Method

aiGust presents the cash flow statement using the **indirect method** for operating activities. This is the most common method for management reporting and aligns with how most accounting software structures its data.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Indirect Method (default)">
    Starts with net profit and works backward to cash by:
    - Adding back non-cash charges (depreciation, amortisation)
    - Adjusting for movements in working capital (receivables, inventory, payables)
    - Removing gains or losses on asset sales (which are reclassified to Investing Activities)

    This method makes the reconciliation between profit and cash explicit, which is valuable for explaining to clients why their bank balance doesn't match their reported profit.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Direct Method (if available)">
    Lists actual cash receipts from customers and cash payments to suppliers and employees as separate line items. Some bookkeeping integrations provide sufficient transaction-level data for aiGust to generate a direct method statement. Where available, toggle to Direct Method using the **Method** selector in the report toolbar.

    <Note>
      Direct method availability depends on how transactions are categorised in the source bookkeeping file. If the toggle is greyed out, the current data structure does not support direct method presentation.
    </Note>
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Identifying Cash Flow Trends

Single-period cash flow figures can be misleading. A month with a large receivable collected will look strong; a month with a tax payment will look weak. Trends over multiple periods reveal the underlying pattern.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Switch to a longer period">
    Set the period selector to **Year** or **Rolling 12 Months** to see the full-year cash flow position. This smooths out the timing differences that distort monthly figures.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Enable period comparison">
    Click **Compare → vs Prior Year** to place the current year's cash flow alongside the prior year. Look for structural shifts: is operating cash flow growing in line with profit? Is the business spending more or less on capital investment?
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review operating cash flow quality">
    A business that consistently reports operating cash flow well below net profit is typically funding its growth through working capital — receivables are growing, inventory is building, or creditors are being paid faster. Identify which working capital line is the main driver.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check free cash flow">
    Subtract investing cash outflows from operating cash inflows to approximate free cash flow — the cash available after maintaining and growing the asset base. A business with strong profit but negative free cash flow is reinvesting heavily; a business with positive free cash flow has capacity to service debt, pay dividends, or build reserves.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Comparing Cash Flow to Profit

The indirect method operating section makes the profit-to-cash reconciliation visible. Understanding it line by line is one of the most useful skills in financial analysis.

<Accordion title="Depreciation and amortisation add-backs">
  Depreciation reduces profit but doesn't consume cash (the cash was spent when the asset was purchased). Adding it back is the first adjustment in the indirect method. A business with high depreciation relative to capital expenditure may be running down its asset base.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Movement in trade receivables">
  If receivables increased during the period, cash collected from customers was less than revenue recognised. This is shown as a negative adjustment to operating cash flow. If receivables decreased, cash collected exceeded revenue — a positive adjustment.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Movement in inventory">
  An inventory build consumes cash without creating a P&L expense in the same period (the cost hits COGS only when goods are sold). An inventory run-down releases cash. Large inventory movements are often where the profit-to-cash gap originates for product-based businesses.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Movement in trade payables">
  If payables increased, the business is effectively using its suppliers to fund operations — it has incurred expenses but hasn't yet paid them. This is a positive adjustment to operating cash flow. Falling payables means the business paid its creditors faster than it incurred new costs, consuming cash.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Tax paid vs tax expense">
  Tax paid (in the financing or operating section, depending on classification) often differs from the tax expense on the P&L due to timing of instalments and assessments. A large tax payment in a period of lower profit can temporarily suppress cash flow.
</Accordion>

<Tip>
  When a client says "I made a profit but I have no cash", open the cash flow report and walk them through the working capital adjustments. Most clients find the indirect method reconciliation genuinely illuminating once it's explained in plain language.
</Tip>

## Using Cash Flow Data in Client Meetings

Cash flow is a topic clients respond to viscerally — it directly affects their ability to pay wages, meet loan repayments, and fund growth. Use aiGust's cash flow report to structure the cash-focused part of any client conversation.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Start with the net movement">
    Open with "cash went up by X / went down by X this period" — this is the number the client knows from their bank account. Confirming it matches gives them confidence in the data.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Show where operating cash flow came from">
    Walk through the operating section. Highlight the profit-to-cash gap if it's material, and explain the one or two working capital lines driving it. For most clients, this is the first time they've seen this analysis.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Explain investing outflows">
    Large investing outflows represent asset purchases. Confirm these align with capital expenditure the client remembers making. If they don't match, there may be a bookkeeping issue.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review financing movements">
    Note any new borrowings or repayments. If the client has been drawing down a line of credit to cover operating shortfalls, the financing section will show this — it's a useful prompt for a conversation about working capital management.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Project forward">
    Use the current period's cash flow pattern to frame a forward-looking question: "If the business continues at this rate, what does cash look like in three months?" aiGust's forecasting tools (available from the **Forecast** tab) can model this directly.
  </Step>
</Steps>
