Ratios turn raw financial figures into comparable signals. A business with $500,000 in current assets means nothing in isolation — but a current ratio of 0.8 tells you immediately that short-term liabilities exceed short-term assets, and that the client may struggle to meet obligations as they fall due. aiGust calculates a comprehensive set of liquidity, solvency, profitability, and efficiency ratios automatically from the synced bookkeeping data, so you can move straight to interpretation without building spreadsheets.

## Where to Find Ratios in aiGust

Ratios are available in three places:

- **Ratios Report** — a dedicated report under the **Reports** menu that shows all available ratios for the selected period, with trend charts and comparison columns.
- **Dashboard KPI cards** — individual ratio cards can be added to the client dashboard from the **Customise** panel.
- **Report sidebars** — the P&L and balance sheet reports surface contextually relevant ratios in the sidebar when a section is expanded (for example, the gross margin percentage appears in the Revenue section sidebar).

## Liquidity Ratios

Liquidity ratios measure the business's ability to meet short-term obligations. They are most relevant for businesses with tight cash cycles or significant short-term debt.

<Accordion title="Current Ratio">
  **Formula:** Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

  Measures whether the business has enough short-term assets to cover its short-term liabilities. A ratio above 1.0 is generally healthy; below 1.0 suggests the business may need to draw on credit or delay payments to meet near-term obligations.

  **Interpretation note:** A very high current ratio (above 3.0) can indicate inefficient use of assets — too much cash sitting idle or excessive inventory. Context matters more than the absolute number.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Quick Ratio (Acid Test)">
  **Formula:** (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities

  A stricter liquidity measure that excludes inventory, which may not convert to cash quickly. For product-based businesses where inventory is a significant current asset, the quick ratio often tells a different story than the current ratio.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Cash Ratio">
  **Formula:** Cash and Cash Equivalents ÷ Current Liabilities

  The most conservative liquidity measure. Shows whether the business could cover all current liabilities using only cash on hand. Most businesses will have a cash ratio well below 1.0 — it becomes relevant when you're assessing a client in financial distress.
</Accordion>

## Solvency Ratios

Solvency ratios measure the business's ability to meet long-term obligations and the degree to which it is funded by debt versus equity.

<Accordion title="Debt-to-Equity Ratio">
  **Formula:** Total Liabilities ÷ Total Equity

  Shows how much of the business is funded by creditors relative to owners. A ratio of 1.0 means debt and equity are equal. Higher ratios indicate greater financial leverage and higher risk if income declines.

  **Interpretation note:** Acceptable debt-to-equity levels vary significantly by industry. Capital-intensive businesses (manufacturing, property) routinely carry higher ratios than service businesses.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Debt-to-Assets Ratio">
  **Formula:** Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets

  Expresses what proportion of assets are financed by debt. A ratio above 0.6 (60%) is often considered high leverage for a small business, though context — particularly the nature of the assets and the predictability of income — matters significantly.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Interest Coverage Ratio">
  **Formula:** EBIT ÷ Interest Expense

  Measures how many times the business can cover its interest expense from operating profit. A ratio below 2.0 warrants attention; below 1.0 means operating profit is insufficient to cover interest — the business is either drawing down reserves or relying on principal repayment holidays.
</Accordion>

## Profitability Ratios

Profitability ratios measure how efficiently the business converts revenue into profit at different stages of the income statement.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Gross Margin %" icon="percent" href="/insights/benchmarking">
    **Formula:** Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100

    The percentage of revenue remaining after direct costs. Benchmarked against industry averages in the **vs Benchmark** comparison. A declining gross margin is one of the most common early warnings of pricing pressure or cost inflation.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Operating Margin %" icon="percent" href="/insights/benchmarking">
    **Formula:** Operating Profit (EBIT) ÷ Revenue × 100

    The percentage of revenue remaining after all operating costs but before interest and tax. Shows how efficiently the business manages its overhead relative to its revenue base.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Net Profit Margin %" icon="percent" href="/insights/benchmarking">
    **Formula:** Net Profit ÷ Revenue × 100

    The percentage of revenue that becomes bottom-line profit. Includes the impact of interest and tax, so it reflects the full cost of the business's capital structure as well as its operating efficiency.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Return on Equity (ROE)" icon="percent" href="/insights/benchmarking">
    **Formula:** Net Profit ÷ Average Total Equity × 100

    Measures the return the business generates for its owners on the equity they have invested. Useful for comparing performance across periods or against benchmark businesses of similar equity base.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## Efficiency Ratios

Efficiency ratios measure how well the business manages its working capital and asset base to generate revenue.

<Accordion title="Debtor Days (Days Sales Outstanding)">
  **Formula:** (Trade Receivables ÷ Revenue) × Number of Days in Period

  The average number of days the business takes to collect payment after a sale. A rising debtor days figure means customers are taking longer to pay — which absorbs cash even if revenue is growing. Compare to the client's stated payment terms: if debtor days are 45 but terms are 30, collections are slipping.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Creditor Days (Days Payable Outstanding)">
  **Formula:** (Trade Payables ÷ COGS) × Number of Days in Period

  The average number of days the business takes to pay its suppliers. A rising creditor days figure can mean the business is strategically extending payment terms, or it can indicate cash flow pressure. Compare to the client's supplier terms for context.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Inventory Days (Days Inventory Outstanding)">
  **Formula:** (Inventory ÷ COGS) × Number of Days in Period

  The average number of days inventory is held before being sold. High inventory days tie up cash and increase the risk of obsolescence. Only applicable for businesses that hold physical stock — aiGust suppresses this ratio for service-based entities.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Cash Conversion Cycle">
  **Formula:** Debtor Days + Inventory Days − Creditor Days

  The number of days between spending cash to produce or buy goods and receiving cash from customers. A shorter cycle is better. A negative cash conversion cycle (common in retail and subscription businesses) means the business collects cash before it pays suppliers — a structurally advantageous position.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Asset Turnover">
  **Formula:** Revenue ÷ Average Total Assets

  Measures how much revenue the business generates for each dollar of assets. Higher is generally better, but the ratio varies significantly by industry. Capital-intensive businesses will always have lower asset turnover than lean service businesses.
</Accordion>

## Interpreting Ratios in Context

A ratio read in isolation can mislead. Apply these principles every time you use ratio analysis:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Compare to a trend, not just a snapshot">
    A current ratio of 1.2 looks fine in isolation. A current ratio that has moved from 2.1 to 1.2 over 12 months looks alarming. Always view ratios in the trend chart context provided by aiGust.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compare to industry benchmarks">
    Enable the **vs Benchmark** comparison on the Ratios Report to see where the client sits relative to industry medians. A gross margin of 35% is strong in retail but weak in professional services. Benchmarks are the reference frame that gives ratios meaning.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Distinguish structural from cyclical changes">
    A seasonal business may have poor liquidity ratios at the trough of its cycle and excellent ones at the peak. Understand the client's business cycle before drawing conclusions from a single period.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Cross-reference ratios against each other">
    Ratios are most powerful in combination. A business with a high net margin but poor cash conversion cycle is generating profit but not cash. A business with a low gross margin but high asset turnover may still be profitable. No single ratio gives the full picture.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Using Ratios in Combination with Benchmarking

The **vs Benchmark** comparison mode in the Ratios Report adds a benchmark column to every ratio. Benchmarks are sourced from industry data segmented by business size (revenue band) and industry classification code.

<Tip>
  When a client's ratio is significantly worse than the benchmark median, use it to open a structured conversation rather than present a problem. "Your debtor days are 52, and the median for businesses like yours is 34 — what's driving the difference?" is a much more productive frame than "your collections are slow."
</Tip>

To get the most from benchmarking:

- Confirm the correct industry code is set for the entity in **Settings → Entity Details**. An incorrect industry code will benchmark against the wrong peer group.
- Use the **P25 / Median / P75** toggle to see the range of benchmark outcomes, not just the median. This helps clients understand whether they're genuinely underperforming or sitting at the lower end of a wide distribution.
- Export the Ratios Report with benchmark columns included when preparing a written advisory report for a client.

## Adding Ratios to Meeting Flows

Ratios work best when anchored to a question the client already cares about.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Before the meeting" icon="clipboard-check" href="/meetings/basics">
    Review the Ratios Report and identify the two or three ratios that have moved most since the last period, or that are furthest from the industry benchmark. Prepare a one-sentence plain-English explanation of each one.
  </Card>
  <Card title="During the meeting" icon="comments" href="/meetings/running-a-meeting">
    Start with the business outcome the client cares about — "cash is tighter than last quarter" — and use the ratio as evidence: "Debtor days have moved from 28 to 44, which means about $X is tied up in uncollected invoices." Ratios as tools, not end points.
  </Card>
  <Card title="After the meeting" icon="file-export" href="/meetings/flows">
    Export the Ratios Report and include a selected subset in your meeting summary or advisory letter. Use the **Custom Selection** export option to include only the ratios you discussed rather than the full list.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Track over time" icon="chart-bar" href="/reports/dashboard">
    Add key ratio cards to the client dashboard so both you and the client can monitor them between meetings. A client who checks their debtor days card weekly develops an intuition for their own business that makes advisory conversations richer.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>
